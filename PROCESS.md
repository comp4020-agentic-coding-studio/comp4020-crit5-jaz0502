# Process overview

## What I built

I built a tennis wall game where the player uses a racquet to hit a ball against a wall, trying to keep the rally going for as long as possible. There are three difficulty levels to choose from. At the hardest level, the ball gradually increases in speed while the racquet becomes smaller with each hit, making it increasingly difficult to return the ball. Moving blockers are also introduced to make the ball's trajectory less predictable. Targets occasionally appear on the wall, giving the player an opportunity to earn bonus points by hitting them. These elements build difficulty and add variety while keeping the core interaction simple: move the racquet to hit the ball. The game is designed to be immediately understandable through its visual affordances, so the player can begin playing without any instructions.


## The moments that mattered

A moment that mattered was testing the game's difficulty. When I first played the game, I found that the ball was not increasing in speed quickly enough, which made the early part of the game feel repetitive and boring. My first response was to increase the ball's speed earlier, but when I combined this with the moving blockers and other difficulty changes, the game became too difficult too quickly. Instead of increasing the ball speed earlier, I used the blockers to add unpredictability while keeping the ball's speed on a more gradual increase. Playing the game helped me recognise that difficulty does not have to come from a single variable constantly increasing; different challenges can work together to create a more gradual progression. I knew the result was better because I could maintain a rally for longer while still feeling that the game was becoming increasingly challenging.
